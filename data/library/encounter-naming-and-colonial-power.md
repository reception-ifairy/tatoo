# Encounter, Naming, Collecting, and Colonial Power in the Global Tattoo Archive

## Abstract

This paper examines how European travel accounts, missionary suppression, ethnographic photography, and museum collecting shaped the very taxonomies this corpus works within — including the English word "tattoo" itself — and how colonial power relations distorted, commodified, and sometimes violently appropriated tattooed bodies as objects of Western study and display. This paper draws together material already established across [From Sailor Flash to Neo-Traditional](/blog/from-sailor-flash-to-neo-traditional), [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), and [Reading Marks That Rarely Survive](/blog/reading-marks-that-rarely-survive) with newly researched material on colonial ethnographic photography and museum collecting practice, arguing that the corpus's own taxonomic categories (a genuine consideration flagged in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s own treatment of "tribal" as a Western-imposed umbrella term) are themselves partly a colonial-era artifact requiring ongoing critical attention rather than a neutral, pre-existing classification scheme onto which cultural content can be safely mapped.

## Terminology and Scope

**The colonial archive (this paper's organizing concept).** The accumulated body of European/Western travel writing, missionary records, ethnographic photography, and museum collections documenting non-Western tattoo traditions — a body of material this paper treats as evidentially valuable (per [Reading Marks That Rarely Survive](/blog/reading-marks-that-rarely-survive)'s evidence classes) but methodologically compromised by the power relations under which it was produced: subjects typically could not consent to or control how they were depicted, named, or subsequently displayed, and the resulting archive reflects the colonizer's descriptive categories and interests as much as the tattooed communities' own self-understanding.

## Historical and Geographic Context

**Naming: the word "tattoo" itself as a colonial-encounter artifact.** As documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 3), the English word "tattoo" derives from the Tahitian/Samoan tatau (Marquesan tatu), entering English via Captain Cook's 1769 voyage and Joseph Banks's journal — meaning the very term this entire thirty-essay research collection uses as its master category is itself a transliteration produced by a specific colonial-encounter moment, not a neutral scientific term independently derived. This paper treats this fact as more than etymological trivia: it illustrates directly how Western taxonomy of a global human practice was seeded by one specific 18th-century encounter, subsequently applied retroactively and expansively to unrelated traditions worldwide (Japanese irezumi, Inuit kakiniit, Aztec/Maya body marking, and others documented across this corpus) that had their own pre-existing names and conceptual frameworks the English word "tattoo" does not carry.

**Missionary suppression across multiple, unconnected traditions.** This paper cross-references the pattern, independently documented across this corpus, of Christian missionary activity suppressing tattoo practice across unconnected regions: Māori tā moko and Marquesan patutiki's colonial-era suppression ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), Section 3); Inuit kakiniit/tunniit's suppression by missionary influence across the Arctic ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), [Before & Beyond the Machine](/blog/before-and-beyond-the-machine), Section 3); and, in a distinct register, Japan's own Meiji-era ban partly motivated by a desire to appear "civilized" to Western powers rather than by direct missionary pressure ([The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum), Section 3) — illustrating that suppression took varied institutional forms (direct missionary prohibition; colonial administrative law; indigenous-government self-westernization) rather than a single uniform colonial mechanism.

**Photography: the Sarawak Museum archive as a case study in ambivalent colonial documentation.** This paper's newly researched material: the Sarawak Museum (Borneo) maintained a photographic archive — including a 1956 photograph of women tattooing at Long Jegan longhouse — originally established under colonial administration (associated with ethnographer Tom Harrisson) primarily to document museum work and provide research/exhibition material. Contemporary scholarship on this archive treats it as methodologically ambivalent rather than simply condemnable: while produced under a colonial ethnographic gaze, the same photographic record has subsequently become, per the archive's own documented contemporary use, "a valuable resource... for members of communities interested in the conservation of their cultural heritage," with ongoing collaborations between Dayak source communities and the museum — an example this paper treats as illustrating that colonial-era documentation, however compromised in its original production context, can be actively reclaimed and repurposed by descendant communities rather than only ever functioning as an instrument of appropriation.

**"Human zoos" and the display of tattooed bodies as spectacle (1878–1889 onward).** This paper's newly researched material: European colonial exhibitions — specifically the 1878 and 1889 Universal Expositions in Paris and the Colonial International Exhibition in Amsterdam — displayed living individuals from colonized non-European cultures within constructed "indigenous village" pavilions for Western public consumption, a practice now widely termed "human zoos" and retrospectively critiqued (including through contemporary artistic responses, e.g., Coco Fusco and Guillermo Gómez-Peña's 1992 performance work *The Year of the White Bear/Two Undiscovered Amerindians*) as having decontextualized and objectified colonized peoples, including in some documented cases tattooed individuals whose body markings became part of the spectacle's "exotic" appeal. This paper treats this practice as the extreme end of a spectrum whose more common, everyday form is the ethnographic photograph or museum specimen label — both sharing the underlying colonial-gaze dynamic of depicting tattooed bodies as objects of Western curiosity rather than as members of self-representing communities.

**Museum collecting and human remains: toi moko and beyond.** As documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 3), the Māori toi moko trade — European collector demand driving the trafficking of preserved, tattooed Māori heads — stands as this corpus's most severe documented instance of colonial museum-collecting violence directly targeting tattooed human remains. This paper connects this to the broader pattern already flagged in [Reading Marks That Rarely Survive](/blog/reading-marks-that-rarely-survive) (Section 9): the Siberian Ice Maiden's (Princess Ukok) repatriation controversy, and the general ethical complexity of major Western and Russian museums (the Hermitage, the British Museum) holding tattooed human remains and associated ethnographic photography collected under colonial or unequal power conditions, an issue contemporary museum studies scholarship (per this paper's newly researched sourcing) frames as requiring active "decolonization" of collections through direct collaboration with, and narrative control returned to, source communities — a shift documented as gaining momentum specifically in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

**Mistranslation and the "tribal" category as a colonial-taxonomic artifact.** This paper's final newly emphasized point, building directly on [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s own definitional work (Section 2): the generic Western category "tribal tattoo" is not a neutral descriptive term but a colonial-era (and post-colonial, 1990s neo-tribal-revival-era) taxonomic flattening of numerous, mutually distinct Indigenous traditions (Polynesian, Bornean/Dayak, and others) into a single undifferentiated Western commercial category — precisely the flattening [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) explicitly warns against reproducing in this corpus's own database structure. This paper treats "tribal" as a case study in how colonial-era and subsequent Western commercial taxonomy can actively obscure the specific, self-naming knowledge systems (per [Pacific Tattoo Knowledge Systems](/blog/pacific-tattoo-knowledge-systems)) that Indigenous communities themselves maintain.

## Social Meaning, Identity, Ritual, and Memory

**Contemporary Indigenous artistic responses to the colonial photographic archive.** This paper's newly researched material documents contemporary Indigenous Australian artists (Brook Andrew, Wiradjuri/Ngunnawal peoples; Christian Thompson, Bidjara people) actively engaging with colonial ethnographic photographic archives (the Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies; the Pitt Rivers Museum) as source material for new artistic work explicitly framed as "truth-telling" and, in Thompson's specific case, "spiritual repatriation" — an approach distinct from either simple rejection or uncritical acceptance of the colonial archive, instead actively reclaiming and recontextualizing it. This paper treats this as a directly relevant model for how this corpus's own AI-support layer might responsibly engage material with a comparably compromised production history (colonial-era photography and museum documentation of tattooed Indigenous individuals) — citing and contextualizing rather than either suppressing the historical record or treating it as neutral raw material.

## Ethics, Cultural Authority, and Restricted Forms

**This entire paper functions as an extended ethical-use consideration for this corpus's treatment of colonially-documented tattoo traditions.** Its central recommendation: wherever this corpus's style papers (particularly [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)) cite colonial-era travel accounts, missionary records, or museum/photographic sources as evidence for a living Indigenous tradition's history, the catalogue should flag that source's colonial production context explicitly (as this paper's own sourcing does) rather than presenting 18th–20th-century European ethnographic material as a neutral, disinterested historical record equivalent in standing to community-authored or community-authorized sources (e.g., Mallon and Galliot's 2018 history, produced in direct collaboration with Sāmoan community knowledge, per [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)). This distinction — between colonial-observer sourcing and community-authored/authorized sourcing — should itself be a field the catalogue tracks, not merely an implicit quality judgment left to the reader.

## Critical Debates and Limits of Evidence

**The Sarawak Museum archive's ambivalent status is itself a genuinely and productively contested scholarly question, not a settled one.** This paper's sourcing explicitly frames the archive as valuable to descendant communities despite its colonial origin, but does not claim this resolves the underlying ethical tension in how the photographs were originally obtained and displayed; this paper treats the archive's value-despite-compromised-origin status as itself the contested finding, consistent with contemporary museum-studies scholarship's own unresolved debates about restitution, display consent, and interpretive authority.

**Source limitations.** This paper's newly researched material (Sarawak Museum archive, human zoos, decolonizing-museums scholarship, Indigenous Australian artistic responses) draws on a mix of academic sources (a Springer Nature-published peer-reviewed article on Brazilian ethnographic collections; a University of Michigan-hosted academic archive essay on the Sarawak Museum; a Harvard undergraduate journal article) and museum-professional sources (ICOM, the International Council of Museums) — a generally strong sourcing profile for a historiographical paper of this kind, though this paper notes that its specific claims about any single named colonial photograph or collection's contemporary community-collaboration status should be verified against the specific museum's own current, updated documentation before being treated as permanently settled, since decolonization efforts are actively ongoing and their specific institutional status changes over time.
