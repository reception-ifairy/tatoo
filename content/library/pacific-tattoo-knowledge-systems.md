# Pacific Tattoo Knowledge Systems: Continuity, Genealogy, Service, Rank, Gender, and Revival

## Abstract

This paper treats Sāmoan tatau, Māori tā moko, Marquesan patutiki, and the wider Pacific tattoo tradition as distinct knowledge systems rather than variations on a single "Polynesian tattoo" theme, centering Indigenous scholarship, language, authority structures, colonial disruption, and contemporary renewal as the organizing framework, per the corpus plan's explicit instruction. This paper is substantially a deepening and anthropological reframing of material already established at length in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Living Lineages), which this paper cross-references extensively rather than duplicates; its original contribution is treating these three (and related) traditions specifically as *knowledge systems* — bodies of genealogical, cosmological, and social information encoded in and transmitted through tattoo practice — rather than primarily as visual/decorative styles, a framing shift with direct consequences for how the catalogue should model these entries' cultural-ownership fields.

## Terminology and Scope

**Knowledge system (as this paper's organizing concept).** A tattoo tradition understood not merely as a decorative style but as an integrated system for transmitting and encoding genealogy (whakapapa), rank, service obligation, gender role, and cosmological understanding — knowledge that is, in the traditions surveyed here, inseparable from *who* may apply it, *how* it is authorized, and *what* authority governs its correct execution. This paper treats Sāmoan tatau, Māori tā moko, and Marquesan patutiki as three distinct knowledge systems (per [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s own definitional boundary), while acknowledging shared broader Pacific/Polynesian continuities ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s polynesian entry) without collapsing the three into an undifferentiated whole.

## Historical and Geographic Context

This paper defers to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 3) for the detailed chronology of Māori tā moko (pre-contact through toi moko-era colonial disruption to 1970s–80s revival), Sāmoan tatau (documented unbroken continuity, Mallon and Galliot's 2018 academic history), and Marquesan patutiki (near-total suppression and catastrophic depopulation, 1980s–90s-onward Marquesan-led revival, the 2019 film *Patutiki*). This paper's specific contribution is foregrounding the *knowledge-system* framing within that already-established chronology: Sāmoan tatau's documented continuity is significant specifically because it represents unbroken transmission of a complete knowledge system (motif meaning, application protocol, hereditary practitioner authority, and social function) rather than merely unbroken visual-style continuity; conversely, Marquesan patutiki's near-total suppression represented a near-total loss of an integrated knowledge system, not merely a decorative tradition, which is why its revival — as documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) — has required Marquesan practitioners to actively reconstruct not just visual motifs but their meanings and application protocols from fragmentary surviving knowledge and colonial-era ethnographic documentation, a qualitatively harder task than reviving a visual style alone.

## Historical Cases and Analytical Lenses

This paper maps to the same catalogue entries as [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (tribal, maori, polynesian, samoan, marquesan) but reframes them specifically through the knowledge-system lens: maori's tā moko as a whakapapa-encoding system requiring tohunga-tā-moko authority to correctly "read" and apply; samoan's tatau as a service-and-rank-encoding system requiring tufuga tā tatau hereditary authority; marquesan's patutiki as a cosmological/ancestral-memory-encoding system currently being actively reconstructed by community-led revival; and polynesian as the broader family within which these distinct systems, and others (Tongan, Hawaiian, and others not treated at the same depth in this corpus), are related but not interchangeable.

## Materials, Tools, and Process

This paper defers entirely to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 5) and [Before & Beyond the Machine](/blog/before-and-beyond-the-machine) (Section 5) for tools (uhi, au, hand-tapped combs) and technical process, adding only the observation that tool and technique in these traditions are themselves part of the knowledge system rather than separable from it — the specific comb, mallet, and rhythm of hand-tapped application in Sāmoan tatau, for instance, is documented ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), Section 8) as integral to the pain-endurance and service meaning the finished tatau carries, meaning technique cannot be extracted from the tradition's meaning-system without altering what the finished tattoo signifies.

## Formal and Visual Analysis

This paper defers to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 6) for the specific visual grammar of each tradition (Māori koru/manaia/moko kauae; Sāmoan pe'a/malu compositional structure; Marquesan patutiki density and figurative motifs), adding the observation that visual grammar in these systems is not freely recombinable decorative vocabulary but is governed by the same knowledge-system authority (tohunga, tufuga) that governs application — a specific arrangement or motif combination may correctly or incorrectly represent a specific genealogy or rank, a constraint absent from purely decorative tattoo styles documented elsewhere in this corpus.

## The Body: Placement, Healing, and Aging

This paper defers to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 7) for placement, pain, and healing documentation (facial moko/moko kauae, full pe'a/malu coverage, ten-to-fifteen-session physical demands), noting that in a knowledge-system framing, placement itself often carries genealogical/status information (which body region receives which pattern) rather than being a purely aesthetic or practical choice — a distinction this paper argues the catalogue's placement-suitability fields should reflect for these three entries specifically, differently from how placement suitability is modeled for purely decorative styles.

## Social Meaning, Identity, Ritual, and Memory

This paper defers to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 8) for the detailed social-meaning documentation already established (whakapapa and mana for Māori; covenant and tautua/service for Sāmoan; ancestral memory and worldview for Marquesan), treating that material as this paper's core substantive content rather than restating it, consistent with this corpus's general practice of cross-referencing rather than duplicating prior papers' authoritative treatment.

## Ethics, Cultural Authority, and Restricted Forms

**This section is this paper's primary original contribution, reframing [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s cultural-ownership findings (Section 9) through the specific lens of knowledge-system authority rather than generic "sacred/restricted" flagging.** Because tā moko, tatau, and patutiki each encode genealogical, service, and cosmological information under specific community-recognized authority structures (tohunga-tā-moko, tufuga tā tatau, and Marquesan community-led revival authority respectively), an AI system generating designs invoking these traditions is not merely risking generic cultural insensitivity but risking the generation of *incorrect or fabricated genealogical/status claims* dressed in authentic-looking visual form — a distinct and, this paper argues, more serious harm than decorative misappropriation alone, since a design falsely implying specific rank, service history, or genealogical descent within these knowledge systems could actively mislead both the wearer and observers within communities where these systems remain living and socially meaningful. This reframing supports and sharpens, rather than contradicts, [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s existing recommendation that the catalogue steer non-community AI-generated requests toward kirituhi-style Māori/Sāmoan-influenced ornament (which makes no genealogical/status claim) rather than purported authentic tā moko or tatau.

## Critical Debates and Limits of Evidence

**No new disputes were identified beyond those already documented at length in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems).** This paper's primary methodological note is that the knowledge-system framing offered here is this paper's own analytical contribution — a way of organizing and foregrounding [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s existing findings for the specific purposes of the corpus's history/anthropology module — rather than a claim sourced to any single new citation. Confidence: high, since this framing is a direct, conservative extension of [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s own extensively sourced findings (Mallon and Galliot 2018; Krutak; multiple museum and community sources) rather than an independent factual claim requiring new verification.
