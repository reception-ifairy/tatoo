# From Sailor Flash to Neo-Traditional: The Genealogy of Western Traditional Tattooing

## Abstract

This paper reconstructs the genealogy of Western "traditional" tattooing and its descendants, providing documented, citation-grounded descriptions for five database catalog entries: American Traditional, Old School, Neo-Traditional, New School, and Vintage/Flash Revival. The central argument is that these five labels are not interchangeable but occupy distinct positions along a chronological and stylistic continuum that runs from maritime folk marking (documented from at least the 1790s), through the codification of a commercial flash-based idiom in the early-to-mid twentieth century, into a late-twentieth-century "renaissance" that produced both illustrative expansions (Neo-Traditional, New School) and a self-conscious revival of the early idiom (Vintage/Flash Revival).

The scope is deliberately restricted to the Western (principally Anglo-American) lineage and its named practitioners and institutions. It does not attempt to document Japanese irezumi, Polynesian tatau, or other non-Western traditions except where they directly shaped the Western styles under discussion. Where dates, attributions, or technical claims are disputed in the literature, the paper flags the conflict explicitly rather than resolving it artificially. The evidentiary basis prioritizes published books by tattoo historians and practitioners, peer-reviewed scholarship, museum and archive holdings, and documented artist biographies; commercial blog content is used sparingly and only for uncontested descriptive claims.

## Terminology and Scope

The five styles are best distinguished along two axes simultaneously: chronology (when the idiom was codified) and visual grammar (line, palette, rendering, and subject matter). Collapsing them into one category — a temptation because they share a common ancestor and a common vocabulary of black outline — obscures precisely the distinctions the catalogue must record.

**Old School.** The broadest and loosest of the terms. It denotes the early-to-mid twentieth-century Western sailor-and-flash idiom generally, and functions primarily as a chronological contrast with "New School." As a temporal label it can encompass European traditional work as well as American work. Its visual grammar: heavy black outlines, a restricted palette (black, red, green, yellow, with limited blue), flat saturated fills, minimal shading, and a compact badge-like scale suited to the forearm or bicep.

**American Traditional.** Denotes the specifically United States lineage of the old-school idiom — the style codified by Cap Coleman, Bert Grimm, Amund Dietzel, Paul Rogers, and Norman "Sailor Jerry" Collins. It shares old school's visual grammar but is defined by national lineage and a specific canonical motif set (anchors, swallows, eagles, roses, daggers, pin-up/lady heads, ships, hearts and banners). In practice most historians and practitioners treat "old school" and "American traditional" as near-synonyms; the distinction is one of emphasis (era versus national style-lineage), not two separate genres.

**Neo-Traditional.** A late-twentieth-century illustrative expansion that retains the structural discipline of traditional work — bold black outlines, a black carbon "barrier" holding color, clarity of composition — but introduces variable line weight, a much wider and more nuanced color palette (mauves, teals, ochres), decorative detailing, and greater dimensional depth. Its acknowledged art-historical borrowings are Art Nouveau and Art Deco ornament together with Japanese ukiyo-e composition. Subject matter is broader (animal portraits, elaborated female portraits, botanicals) and scale is typically larger.

**New School.** A parallel late-twentieth-century expansion, distinguished from Neo-Traditional principally by its cartoonish exaggeration, its extremely bright and unrestricted palette (including neon and acid tones), its distorted/magnified proportions, and its subject matter drawn from graffiti, comics, animation, video games, and hip-hop culture. It retains the heavy outline of old school but abandons realistic proportion.

**Vintage/Flash Revival.** Not a distinct historical era but a contemporary (roughly 1990s–present) self-conscious return to the early-twentieth-century flash idiom. It reproduces the American Traditional visual grammar with deliberate historical accuracy — period-correct palettes, period motifs, hand-painted flash sheets — as an act of revival and homage rather than innovation. It is the "new old style" identified by art historian Matt Lodder.

The critical boundary criteria are therefore: (1) chronology of codification; (2) line-weight discipline (uniform heavy outline in Old School/American Traditional/Vintage; variable in Neo-Traditional; heavy but distorted in New School); (3) palette breadth (restricted in the traditional cluster; expanded-nuanced in Neo-Traditional; expanded-garish in New School); and (4) intent (functional badge in the traditional cluster; illustrative expansion in Neo/New School; deliberate historical homage in Vintage/Flash Revival).

## Historical and Geographic Context

**Maritime folk substrate (c. 1780s–1870s).** The documented substrate of Western tattooing is maritime. Ira Dye's study of United States Seamen's Protection Certificates from 1796 to 1818 — the single most important primary-source study of the period — records that among tattooed American seafarers, initials, names, words, and letters were "the most common and sought after tattoos with 38% of all reported from 1796–1818 falling into this category," followed by sea-themed imagery: "the 21% of sailors who reported some type of sea-themed tattoo from simple anchors and compasses to more complex ships, mermaids, and sea creatures," then symbols of love ("coming in at 8% were symbols of love with around 200 tattoos"), patriotic/political motifs, and religious symbols (about 8%). These were applied at sea by hand, using sewing needles and inks made from soot or gunpowder, long before professional shops existed. This establishes the motif vocabulary — anchors, hearts, names, patriotic emblems, religious symbols — that later flash artists would codify.

**Technological rupture: the electric machine (1891).** Samuel F. O'Reilly, a New York tattooer working on the Bowery, patented the first electric tattoo machine (U.S. Patent No. 464,801) on December 8, 1891, adapting the rotary mechanism of Thomas Edison's autographic printing pen; as later U.S. patent literature summarizes it, "O'Reilly adapted Edison's device for tattooing by adding a tubular handle for delivering ink from an ink reservoir to the perforating needle." Per the Smithsonian Institution's "A Revolution in Tattooing," "even the most proficient tattoo artist could only puncture the skin two or three times a second compared to the tattoo machine's 50 punctures per second," making tattooing faster, cheaper, and more widely available. This technological rupture enabled the commercial street-shop model on which all subsequent Western styles depend. O'Reilly's patent was disputed in court by his former associate Elmer Getchell around 1899–1900, with no clear resolution.

**Codification through commercial flash (c. 1900–1930).** The codification of a shared American idiom was driven by the commercial production and exchange of flash. Albert "Lew the Jew" Alberts (born Albert Morton Kurzman, 1880–1954), a New York wallpaper designer who learned tattooing during Spanish–American War service in the Philippines, is credited as the first to design and market printed flash sheets to other tattooers, around 1905, from the Bowery. His designs "codified the repertoire of American tattooing," in the words of Don Ed Hardy, whose private collection and 2015 book documented Alberts and inspired the 2018–2019 Contemporary Jewish Museum exhibition "Lew the Jew and His Circle." (Bert Grimm told a competing story in which Alberts bought up Charlie Western's flash in Portland and resold the designs as his own; the true version, as the Tattoo Archive notes, "may never be known.") Acetate/celluloid stencils and mailed "rubs" allowed designs to circulate nationally among a small, secretive community — by the mid-twentieth century, Hardy estimates, perhaps 500 tattooers worked in the entire United States.

**Military and port geography (WWI–Korea).** The style matured in naval and military port hubs. Cap Coleman worked East Main Street in Norfolk, Virginia, from 1918, serving the thousands of servicemen drawn by the World War I naval buildup. Amund Dietzel worked Milwaukee from 1913, tattooing Great Lakes Naval Training Station recruits. Bert Grimm ran shops in St. Louis and, most famously, on The Pike/Nu Pike amusement zone in Long Beach, California, tattooing sailors before they shipped out. Sailor Jerry Collins settled on Honolulu's Hotel Street, the shore-leave hub of the Pacific fleet. The two World Wars and the Korean War produced the demand that standardized the badge-like, quickly-executed tattoo.

**Circus and sideshow transmission.** A parallel transmission channel was the traveling carnival and circus. The term "flash" itself derives from the carnival trade, where an eye-catching display was called "flash." Dietzel and William Grimshaw toured as tattooed men in sideshows, tattooing between performances; Grimm spent a season with Buffalo Bill's Wild West Show; Coleman's family was reportedly of carnival background. The sideshow "tattooed attraction" and the street shop were the two economic engines of early professional tattooing.

**Renaissance and expansion (c. 1960s–1990s).** From the 1960s a "tattoo renaissance" (the term used by anthropologist Arnold Rubin) transformed the craft as art-school-trained practitioners reframed tattooing as fine art. Don Ed Hardy — who declined a Yale graduate fellowship, trained under Phil Sparrow (Samuel Steward), corresponded with Sailor Jerry, and studied in Japan from 1973 — opened the first appointment-only custom shop, Realistic Tattoo, in San Francisco in 1974. Margo DeMello argues that in this period elite tattooists and organizations repackaged tattooing for middle-class consumption, shifting iconography from Americana and biker work toward Celtic, tribal, and Japanese imagery. Out of this ferment came the illustrative expansions (Neo-Traditional and New School, both associated with West Coast artists such as Marcus Pacheco) and, dialectically, the flash revival.

**Revival (c. 1990–present).** Lodder dates the self-conscious "new old style" to a shift documented in a 1990 letter from Ed Hardy predicting that "the new old style is what will be happening." Over the 1990s and 2000s, after the eclecticism of the 1970s–80s, large numbers of Western artists returned to the codified traditional idiom, now framed nostalgically as heritage. This revival is the referent of the Vintage / Flash Revival catalog entry.

## Practices and Visual Languages

### American Traditional
The United States lineage of bold, readable, flash-based tattooing codified between roughly the 1900s and 1950s. Foundational figures: Lew Alberts (flash codification, c. 1905); Cap Coleman (Norfolk, from 1918, "Godfather of American Tattooing"); Amund Dietzel (Milwaukee, from 1913); Bert Grimm ("grandfather of old school," St. Louis and Long Beach); Paul Rogers (Coleman's protégé from 1945, machine builder and bridge to the modern renaissance); and Norman "Sailor Jerry" Collins (Honolulu), who synthesized American motifs with Japanese scale and composition. Visual grammar: uniform heavy black outline, restricted primary palette, flat saturated fills, minimal black shading, badge scale. Canonical motifs: anchors, swallows, eagles, roses, daggers, hearts and banners ("Mom," "Death Before Dishonor"), pin-up/lady heads, ships, snakes, panthers.

### Old School
The broader chronological label for the same early-to-mid-twentieth-century idiom, functioning primarily as the antonym of "New School." As a temporal term it can include European traditional work. Bert Grimm is dubbed the "grandfather of old school." Visually identical in its core grammar to American Traditional (heavy outline, restricted palette, flat fills), the term emphasizes era rather than national lineage. The two terms are treated as near-synonymous by most practitioners and historians; the catalogue should record them as closely related but distinguish "old school" as the era-contrast term and "American Traditional" as the style-lineage term.

### Neo-Traditional
A late-twentieth-century illustrative evolution retaining traditional structural rules (bold black outlines, color-holding black barrier, compositional clarity) while introducing variable line weight, an expanded and nuanced palette, decorative detailing, and dimensional depth. Art-historical borrowings: Art Nouveau (flowing organic line, floral framing, filigree), Art Deco (geometric ornament, symmetry), and Japanese ukiyo-e. Dating is disputed: sources variously place its emergence in the 1970s–80s or the late 1980s–90s; San Francisco artists including Marcus Pacheco are named as forerunners. Subject matter: animal portraits, elaborated female portraits, botanicals, mythological and folkloric imagery. Scale: typically larger, well-suited to sleeves.

### New School
A late-twentieth-century style characterized by heavy outlines, extremely vivid and unrestricted color, exaggerated/distorted proportions, and cartoonish or graffiti-influenced subject matter drawn from comics, animation, video games, and hip-hop culture. Origins are debated: some accounts trace West Coast experimentation to the 1970s; most locate its true emergence in the late 1980s–1990s, with a popularity boom in the 1990s fueled by pop-culture nostalgia and internet access. Marcus Pacheco is widely credited as an early popularizer; other associated artists include Joe Capobianco and Jesse Smith. New School also marks a cultural shift toward openness in sharing techniques, in contrast to the secrecy of the old-school trade.

### Vintage / Flash Revival
A contemporary (c. 1990s–present) self-conscious revival reproducing the early-twentieth-century American Traditional idiom with deliberate historical accuracy — period-correct palettes, period motifs, hand-painted flash sheets displayed on shop walls. It is a revival and homage movement rather than a distinct historical era, driven partly by nostalgia and by the durability argument ("bold will hold"). Lodder theorizes it as the "new old style," noting the paradox of a style that is simultaneously old and new, and analyzing the gap between the actual history of the designs and the nostalgic lens through which contemporary artists and collectors view them. Anchoring reference collections include reissued flash of Sailor Jerry, Coleman, Dietzel, and Alberts.

## Materials, Tools, and Process

**Hand methods.** The maritime substrate was hand-applied: sewing needles (sailors were skilled with needles for sailmaking) and inks made from soot, gunpowder, or India ink. Herman Melville, who served in the U.S. Navy in 1843–44, recorded shipmates who "excelled in tattooing, or pricking, as it is called in a man-of-war."

**Electric machines.** O'Reilly's 1891 machine (Patent No. 464,801), derived from Edison's autographic pen, established the reciprocating electromagnetic principle still used today. Percy Waters of Detroit ran what was likely the world's largest tattoo-supply company from roughly 1918 to 1939 and received U.S. Patent No. 1,724,812 for an "Electric Tattooing Device" on August 13, 1929 — a two-coil frame with a fingertip switch and spark shield widely regarded as the design most resembling the modern machine; trade sources credit him with designing fourteen frame styles, though this figure derives from trade consensus rather than peer-reviewed scholarship. Waters' 1929 patent is often described as the only U.S. tattoo-machine patent for roughly fifty years, until Carol Nightingale's in 1979. Sailor Jerry innovated needle groupings that embedded pigment with less skin trauma; he is described as one of the first to use single-use needles and an autoclave for sterilization, though these claims rest largely on trade lore and legacy-brand material rather than independent archival verification.

**Flash and stencils.** Designs were displayed as painted flash sheets and reproduced onto skin via celluloid/acetate stencils: a needle in a pin-vice scratched the design into ~0.020-inch acetate, charcoal was rubbed into the groove, and the powder was transferred to Vaseline-coated skin. Cutting stencils was an apprentice task said to toughen the hands. Cellulose acetate was preferred; the flammable cellulose nitrate variety caused at least one documented shop fire (Sailor George Fosdick's, Portland). Flash was traditionally painted with India ink outlines and watercolor or diluted tattoo ink on Bristol board or watercolor paper. Milton Zeis of Rockford, Illinois, ran a major mail-order supply business from the 1930s, was among the first to sell colored flash and clip cords, and published the 20-lesson Zeis School of Tattooing correspondence course (first issued 1951, priced at $125, accredited by the State of Illinois).

**Pigments.** The earliest inks were black (carbon/soot). The palette expanded — by consensus, red second, then green, brown, and yellow — as tattooers sourced industrial pigments from chemical-company catalogs, often concealing the intended use behind fake company letterheads. Dietzel and Grimshaw are documented using carbon black, "China red" (vermilion), "Casali's green" (viridian), Prussian blue, and a yellow that may have been arylide yellow. Percy Waters and later Milton Zeis commercialized pigment supply. The frequently repeated claim that Sailor Jerry "invented" purple/carbazole violet is misleading: carbazole violet is C.I. Pigment Violet 23, a German industrial pigment patented by Hoechst around 1928 and first marketed around 1952–53; Collins is best described as an early adopter who introduced this violet to tattoo use, not its chemical inventor.

## Formal and Visual Analysis

**American Traditional / Old School.** Line: single heavy, uniform-weight black outline, typically two to four millimeters for primary elements, chosen so the contour remains crisp as dermal pigment softens over decades. Value: minimal, achieved with solid black and occasional black "whip" shading; no smooth gradients. Color: restricted primaries (red, yellow, green, black, limited blue), applied as flat, even, ungraded fills. Composition: single-subject badges with clear negative space, front-facing or profile presentation, banners for text. Motifs: the canonical maritime/military/love/patriotic/religious set. Scale: compact, sized to forearm, bicep, or chest panel.

**Neo-Traditional.** Line: variable weight, retaining a bold outer contour but adding interior line hierarchy. Value: genuine dimensional shading and midtones within the outline. Color: greatly expanded and nuanced — mauves, teals, ochres, "Victorian velvet" tones — with layered transitions. Composition: denser, more ornamental, with decorative framing (filigree, lace, pearls, gems). Motifs: elaborated portraits (especially women with stylized hair), animals, botanicals, folklore. Scale: larger, sleeve-oriented.

**New School.** Line: heavy but dynamic, mixing thick and thin, with fluid movement. Value: strong contrast, sometimes with rendered depth. Color: maximal — bright, saturated, including neon/acid tones. Composition: exaggerated perspective, "bubble" forms, jagged graffiti energy, magnified features (oversized eyes, distorted proportion). Motifs: cartoon and pop-culture characters, graffiti lettering, fantasy. Scale: large, favoring sleeves and back pieces.

**Vintage/Flash Revival.** Deliberately reproduces the American Traditional grammar above with period accuracy, often replicating specific historical flash designs; its distinguishing feature is intent (historical fidelity) rather than a new visual grammar.

## The Body: Placement, Healing, and Aging
The traditional idiom's visual grammar is functionally adapted to the skin as a medium and to how tattoos age. The industry maxim "bold will hold" encapsulates the core principle: thick black outlines and solid, saturated fills retain legibility over decades, whereas fine lines and ungraded delicate color blur and fade as the dermis remodels. Black (carbon) pigment is the longest-lasting in skin; without a black barrier, color is more prone to spread and lose shape. Heavy outlines (two to four millimeters) are chosen precisely because they remain crisp as pigment migrates slightly over time. The restricted palette also reflects longevity: red and yellow fade faster than black and blue and are the most vulnerable to UV degradation.

Placement in the traditional idiom followed both practical and cultural logic: forearms, biceps, chest, and hands were visible, quickly executed, and suited to badge-scale single subjects — appropriate to a walk-in, high-volume port-shop economy. Larger Neo-Traditional and New School work migrated to sleeves and back pieces as tattooing shifted toward custom, appointment-based practice. Dietzel is documented tattooing identification (names, Social Security numbers) discreetly on inner biceps, and covering vaccination marks and scars with rosebuds or butterflies — an early cosmetic/cover-work function. Technical limitations of the early period included the restricted available pigment chemistry, the trauma of multiple needle passes for solid fills, and the risk of infection before sterilization became standard — a risk Sailor Jerry and others addressed through autoclave use and single-use needles.

## Social Meaning, Identity, Ritual, and Memory
For its originating clientele — sailors, soldiers, and marines — the traditional tattoo functioned as a portable, permanent service record and identity marker. A body of sailor lore assigns specific meanings to motifs: an anchor for an Atlantic crossing or merchant-marine service; a swallow for a set distance traveled — per the U.S. Naval Institute's *Naval History Magazine* (Aug. 2022, "Earning Your Ink"), "a swallow tattoo is used [to] measure how far a sailor travels. Originally, a swallow was earned every 5,000 nautical miles" — also symbolizing safe return home; a fully-rigged ship for rounding Cape Horn; a golden dragon for crossing the International Date Line; "Hold Fast" across the knuckles as an occupational charm; pigs and roosters on the feet as anti-drowning talismans; nautical stars for navigational guidance.

These meanings must be reported with scholarly caution. Historian Albert Parry, in his 1933 book, reported such codes as "rigidly adhered to," but tattoo artist Samuel Steward noted Parry published stories without verification, and historian B.R. Burg found no records of tattooed dragons among U.S. Navy sailors earlier than shortly before World War I. Matt Lodder's dictum — per Wikipedia's "Sailor tattoo," "Matt Lodder says that 'tattooing largely reflects the visual culture from which it emerges', including tattoos chosen by Western sailors" — frames these motifs less as a fixed secret code than as an evolving folk vocabulary. The "Mom" heart and the sweetheart's name banner expressed memory and attachment across separation; patriotic eagles and flags expressed national identity; religious motifs (crucifixes) sometimes carried the specific intent of ensuring Christian burial if the wearer died away from home. The circus/sideshow context supplied a parallel meaning: the fully tattooed body as spectacle and livelihood.

## Ethics, Cultural Authority, and Restricted Forms
The core Western traditional idiom — anchors, roses, eagles, pin-ups, daggers — is a commercial folk-art vocabulary without sacred or ceremonially restricted status; it carries no indigenous cultural-ownership restrictions and may be used freely. This is the general case for American Traditional, Old School, and Vintage / Flash Revival.

Two ethical qualifications apply. First, the traditional and revival idioms incorporate motifs simplified from Japanese irezumi (dragons, koi) and, in the wider renaissance, from Polynesian and other Indigenous traditions; critics have argued that streamlining sacred or narrative designs into mass-reproducible flash commodifies and dilutes those traditions, a debate that continues into the present. Where Neo-Traditional or New School work draws directly on living Indigenous or sacred iconographies, the ethical considerations proper to those source traditions apply. Second, DeMello's critique — that the tattoo "renaissance" repackaged a working-class practice for middle-class consumption while borrowing an "exotic, primitive" veneer from non-Western cultures — is itself an ethical/appropriation argument about how the Western tradition has represented its borrowings. For the five catalog entries as such, no sacred or restricted status attaches to the core Americana vocabulary; restrictions arise only at the points of cross-cultural borrowing noted here.

## Critical Debates and Limits of Evidence
**Terminological dispute.** "Old School" and "American Traditional" are used interchangeably by most sources but are not perfectly coextensive: "old school" is the broader era-contrast term (opposed to "new school") and can include European work, while "American Traditional" specifies the U.S. lineage. Confidence: high that they are near-synonyms; the fine distinction is one of emphasis.

**Dating disputes.** The emergence of both Neo-Traditional and New School is genuinely contested. New School is variously dated to the 1970s (West Coast experimentation) or the late 1980s–90s (true emergence, 1990s boom). Neo-Traditional is similarly placed in the 1970s–80s or the late 1980s–90s. The paper reports both ranges rather than adjudicating. Confidence: moderate; the contest is inherent to a craft whose early history was poorly documented and closely guarded.

**Biographical disputes.** Early tattoo history is thick with legend. Bert Grimm's claimed clients (Bonnie and Clyde, Pretty Boy Floyd) are unverified trade lore; his start date is variously given as 1916 or 1927. The Lew Alberts flash-origination story is contradicted by Grimm's counter-narrative. Cap Coleman's birth year (c. 1884), birthplace, and death date (October 1973) carry documentary inconsistencies noted even in sympathetic sources. Confidence: low-to-moderate on specific biographical particulars; high on the broad outlines of who did what.

**Contested technical claims.** Sailor Jerry's "invention" of purple/carbazole violet is contradicted by pigment chemistry (PV23 is a German industrial pigment predating his use); his sterilization and single-use-needle priority rests on trade lore. Confidence: high that he popularized rather than invented the violet; moderate on the sterilization priority.

**Regional variation.** East Coast (Norfolk/Bowery), Midwest (Milwaukee/St. Louis), West Coast (Long Beach/San Francisco), and Pacific (Honolulu) each developed local shop cultures; the West Coast later became the crucible of both the renaissance and the illustrative expansions. Confidence: high.

**Source limitations.** Much of the specialist history originates from a small number of dedicated but non-peer-reviewed sources (the Tattoo Archive; practitioner memoirs; museum exhibition texts) and from a legacy-brand (Sailor Jerry Ltd.) with commercial interest in its subject's mythology. Peer-reviewed scholarship (Dye, DeMello, Rubin, Lodder, Sanders) is strongest on the social history and weakest on granular attribution. The paper weights these accordingly.
