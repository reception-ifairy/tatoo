# Before and Beyond the Machine: Hand Poke, Tebori, Hand-Tapped Tatau, and Skin Stitching

## Abstract

This paper documents four catalog technique entries unified by sharing no machine at all: Hand Poke, Tebori, Hand-Tapped/Tatau, and Skin Stitching. Consistent with the corpus plan's explicit instruction, this paper compares these pre-electric and living manual technologies "without treating them as interchangeable," documenting each one's distinct tools, gestures, apprenticeship structure, ritual authority, pain profile, healing characteristics, and community ownership separately, since — unlike the largely technique-level papers preceding this one in the corpus — three of these four entries (tebori, Hand-Tapped (Tatau), Skin Stitching) are inseparable from the living cultural traditions documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) and [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum), and their technical description cannot be responsibly separated from that cultural context.

## Terminology and Scope

**Hand Poke.** Manual, dot-by-dot tattoo application using a single hand-held needle (no rod, comb, or stitching thread), foundational to the contemporary Western Handpoke/Stick-and-Poke style ([Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions)) and to Ignorant style's DIY execution ([Black as Structure](/blog/black-as-structure), [The Experimental Body](/blog/the-experimental-body)). The most technologically minimal of the four entries, requiring only a needle and ink.

**Tebori.** The specific Japanese hand-carving method: needles bundled and mounted on a bamboo or metal rod (nomi), worked by hand in a repeated levering/poking motion, documented at length in [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) as historically inseparable from Irezumi and prized for a distinctive soft color gradation practitioners describe as unattainable by machine.

**Hand-Tapped/Tatau.** The Polynesian method: pigment tapped into the skin using a comb-like tool (au in Samoan; similar tools across the wider Polynesian tradition) struck rhythmically with a mallet, documented at length in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) as central to Samoan, Māori, and Marquesan tattooing, carrying documented hereditary-practitioner and ritual-authority structures unlike any other entry in this paper.

**Skin Stitching.** The Inuit/Arctic method: a needle (traditionally bone, ivory, or bird bone) threaded with soot-pigmented sinew, drawn beneath the skin so the thread deposits a continuous line of pigment as it passes through — the only entry in this paper that uses thread rather than direct needle-and-ink deposition, documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 2–3) as part of the kakiniit/tunniit Inuit tattooing tradition.

The critical boundary the catalogue must distinguish: Hand Poke (Stick & Poke) is a generic, culturally unattached technique (usable within any style, including the Western DIY revival documented in [Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions)); tebori and Hand-Tapped (Tatau) are specific, named, culturally embedded techniques inseparable from Japanese and Polynesian tradition respectively, each with its own distinct tool, apprenticeship, and (for Hand-Tapped (Tatau) specifically) hereditary/gendered authority structure; and Skin Stitching is technically unique among all four for using a threaded needle rather than direct needle-tip pigment deposition.

## Historical and Geographic Context

**Hand poke's universal priority and specific contemporary revival.** As documented in [Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions) (Section 3), hand-poke-adjacent technique is the technologically prior universal tattoo method, evidenced on the 5,300-year-old mummy Ötzi the Iceman, predating the 1891 electric machine ([From Sailor Flash to Neo-Traditional](/blog/from-sailor-flash-to-neo-traditional)) across every tattooing culture on earth. Its specific contemporary Western named revival ("stick-and-poke") is documented in [Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions) as dating to 1970s–1980s punk/hardcore subculture, with a further 2010s social-media-driven professionalization.

**Tebori's Edo-period-to-present continuity.** As documented in [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) (Section 3, 5), tebori is the traditional technique of Japanese irezumi, practiced by horishi across the Edo period and into the present; contemporary master Horiyoshi III (b. 1946) performed outlining entirely by hand until adopting electric machine outlining in the late 1990s (influenced by his friendship with Don Ed Hardy, begun mid-1980s) while retaining tebori specifically for shading and color — a hybrid approach [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) documents as now standard among many prominent contemporary horishi.

**Hand-tapped tatau's documented unbroken continuity and hereditary structure.** As documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 3, 5), Samoan tatau is distinguished among Pacific tattoo traditions by unbroken Indigenous continuity since before European contact, applied exclusively by hereditary tufuga tā tatau from specific family lines (Sā Su'a, Sā Tulou'ena per [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s citation of Mallon and Galliot's 2018 academic history), using an au (comb-like tool, traditionally boar-tusk, bone, or turtle-shell toothed, mounted on a wooden handle) struck with a mallet. Māori tā moko's uhi-chisel method and Marquesan patutiki's comparable hand-tapped technique are documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) as sharing this broad Polynesian hand-tapped technology family while maintaining island-specific tool and protocol distinctions.

**Skin stitching's Arctic-wide, near-suppressed, actively revived history.** This paper's newly researched material substantially deepens [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s brief treatment of Skin Stitching. The technique — a needle (bone, ivory, or bird bone, with bird bone specifically preferred by some practitioners for its flexibility, per Inuit needle-case expert Krista Zawadski) threaded with sinew (caribou or whale) soaked in soot-based pigment (mixed with seal oil or urine, per University of Alaska archaeologist Otto W. Geist's 1926 ethnographic account) and drawn beneath the skin — was practiced across the Inuit Nunaat (Alaska, Canada, Greenland) for a documented span of at least 10,000 years by some accounts, most commonly by and among women, marking life achievements and milestones. The practice was suppressed by Christian missionary influence during the 19th–20th century Western-contact period across the Arctic. Contemporary revival is documented through named practitioners: Kalaaleq (West Greenlandic) tattooer-researcher Maya Sialuk Jacobsen, who grew up in Qeqertarsuaq, Greenland, and — after training in Western machine tattooing — deliberately set aside her tattoo machine to specialize exclusively in hand-poke and skin-stitching technique, learning partly by practicing on herself and her husband and describing skin stitching specifically as painful; and Gwich'in artist Jeneen Frei Njootli (Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation, Old Crow, Yukon), documented in a 2016 CBC News profile as having completed a cultural tattoo-training residency specifically to reclaim skin-stitching practice, framing the work explicitly as "re-matriating these lines back onto our bodies."

## Technical Studies

### Hand Poke (Stick & Poke)
Manual, single-needle, dot-by-dot application with no machine, technologically prior to all machine tattooing (evidenced from Ötzi the Iceman onward) and revived as a specifically named Western style from 1970s–80s punk subculture ([Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions)).

### Tebori
Japanese hand-carving method using a bundle of needles on a bamboo/metal rod (nomi), historically the sole method of executing Irezumi, now typically used in hybrid combination with machine outlining by contemporary masters including Horiyoshi III ([The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum)).

### Hand-Tapped (Tatau)
Polynesian comb-and-mallet method (au in Samoan), documented as having produced unbroken continuous practice in Samoan tatau specifically, applied under hereditary tufuga authority; the Māori (uhi) and Marquesan variants share this broad technology family with island-specific tools and protocols ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)).

### Skin Stitching
Inuit/Arctic method using a threaded needle (bone/ivory/bird-bone, sinew thread, soot pigment) drawn beneath the skin, practiced for at least 10,000 years by some accounts, suppressed by 19th–20th-century missionary influence, and actively revived today by named practitioners including Maya Sialuk Jacobsen and Jeneen Frei Njootli.

## Materials, Tools, and Process

**Hand poke's minimal tool requirement.** A single needle (contemporary professional practice uses sterile, purpose-made single-needle tools; historical/DIY practice has used sewing needles, sometimes bound to a stick or pencil) dipped directly in ink, poked repeatedly into the skin by hand ([Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions), Section 5).

**Tebori's rod-and-bundle construction.** A rod (bamboo historically, sometimes metal today) with a bundle of needles fixed at one end, worked by the horishi holding the client's skin taut with one hand while driving the rod with the other in a repeated levering/poking motion, producing the characteristic sound from which practitioners describe the technique; mastery is documented in [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) as requiring longer to achieve than machine competency given the number of simultaneously controlled variables (angle, speed, pressure, timing, interval).

**Hand-tapped's comb-and-mallet construction.** The au (Samoan) — a hand-carved, toothed comb (traditionally boar tusk, bone, or turtle shell) mounted on a wooden handle — is struck rhythmically with a mallet to tap pigment (traditionally burned candlenut soot) into the skin; a full pe'a or malu is documented in [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 5) as commonly requiring ten to fifteen sessions given the physical demands of hand-tapped application over such extensive coverage, with technique adapting as the skin swells across sessions.

**Skin stitching's threaded-needle construction — technically unique among all four entries.** Unlike the other three techniques in this paper, skin stitching does not deposit pigment via direct needle-tip contact; instead, a needle (bone, ivory, or sharpened bird bone — bird-bone needles specifically preferred by some practitioners for greater flexibility) is threaded with sinew (caribou or whale) that has been soaked in a soot-based pigment (mixed with seal oil or, per some historical accounts, urine), and the pigmented thread is drawn beneath the skin's surface, depositing a continuous line of pigment as the thread passes through — a mechanically distinct process from every other tattooing technique documented across this entire thirty-essay research collection. A related but distinct "poke-and-tap" variant (a sharp bone tool dipped in pigment and tapped to create individual dots rather than continuous threaded lines) is also documented for some Inupiat practice, functioning more like Hand Poke (Stick & Poke) technique within the same broader Arctic tradition.

**Contemporary hygiene adaptation across all four techniques.** Reflecting this corpus's [The Constructed Line](/blog/the-constructed-line) documentation of OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030), contemporary practitioners of all four techniques have adapted traditional materials for sterility: Maya Sialuk Jacobsen uses metal needles and cotton thread rather than traditional bone and sinew specifically to ensure sterilization; a 2025 Canadian public-health review of traditional Indigenous tattoo methods notes that threads made from animal sinew or gut are difficult to sterilize, and that some practitioners prefer traditional materials for cultural-authenticity reasons even as pre-sterilized synthetic threads mimicking sinew are offered as a compromise between safety and cultural fidelity; the same review documents a 2022 case in Nunavut in which a woman was injured during traditional tattooing with a narwhal-tusk needle, leaving a bone fragment in her skin — an incident cited as illustrating genuine, non-hypothetical safety tension between traditional-material authenticity and modern sterile practice.

## Formal and Visual Analysis

This paper defers to the style papers for each technique's associated visual outcome: Hand Poke (Stick & Poke)'s characteristic soft, slightly irregular dot-built lines ([Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions), Section 6); tebori's distinctive soft, luminous color gradation ([The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum), Section 6); Hand-Tapped (Tatau)'s dense geometric band compositions across Samoan, Māori, and Marquesan traditions ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), Section 6); and Skin Stitching's continuous fine lines, documented in this paper's new research as producing "intricate lines" (per the Hakai Magazine account of a facial tattoo applied via bird-bone-needle skin stitching) distinct in character from tebori's or hand-tapped's broader coverage.

## The Body: Placement, Healing, and Aging

**Comparative pain and trauma profiles.** Hand_poke is documented ([Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions), Section 7) as generally involving less immediate trauma than machine tattooing; by contrast, skin stitching is explicitly and repeatedly described by contemporary practitioners (Maya Sialuk Jacobsen specifically) as painful, and hand-tapped tatau's extensive, multi-session, full-body-coverage application ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), Section 7) is documented as a deliberate physical endurance test integral to its social meaning rather than merely an incidental discomfort.

**Facial and high-visibility placement across traditions.** Skin stitching's documented historical application to Inuit women's facial tattoos (tunniit, per [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)) and hand-tapped tatau's application to Māori facial moko and historical Marquesan/Samoan facial tattooing ([Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems), Section 7) both place some of this paper's most culturally significant work on the most socially visible and permanent body location, a placement choice inseparable from both traditions' function as public, permanent declarations of identity and status.

**Sterilization tension unique to Skin Stitching and Hand-Tapped (Tatau).** As documented in Section 5 above, the specific materials traditionally used in skin stitching (sinew/gut thread) and hand-tapped tatau (bone/turtle-shell combs) present genuine, documented sterilization challenges distinct from tebori's and Hand Poke (Stick & Poke)'s comparatively simpler single-needle sterilization requirements, requiring practitioners to navigate a real tension between traditional-material cultural authenticity and modern infection-control standards.

## Social Meaning, Identity, Ritual, and Memory

This paper defers substantially to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) and [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) for the deep cultural meaning attached to tebori and Hand-Tapped (Tatau) specifically (gaman/endurance for tebori, per [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) Section 8; whakapapa/mana, covenant/service, and ancestral memory for Hand-Tapped (Tatau)'s Māori, Samoan, and Marquesan applications, per [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) Section 8). This paper's new contribution is Skin Stitching's specific social meaning: contemporary revival practitioners frame the technique explicitly in terms of cultural reclamation and "re-matriation" (Jeneen Frei Njootli's own term, per CBC News) — a specifically gendered reclamation given the tradition's documented historical association with women practitioners and wearers — and as a means of "connecting" and "strengthening community" following a suppression Frei Njootli and other contemporary practitioners experience as a loss actively being repaired rather than as settled history.

## Ethics, Cultural Authority, and Restricted Forms

**Hand_poke, in its generic Western DIY-revival form, carries no cultural-ownership consideration** distinct from what [Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions) (Section 9) already documents, though the catalogue should apply [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s sensitivity guidance whenever a design specifically invokes a living Indigenous hand-tapped or skin-stitched tradition rather than the generic punk-revival aesthetic.

**Tebori carries the attribution-not-restriction consideration already documented in [The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum) (Section 9):** not sacred or formally restricted, but warranting accurate representation of its apprenticeship lineage and technical demands rather than surface-level pastiche.

**Hand_tapped carries the strongest restriction-level consideration of any technique in this paper, inherited directly from [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems) (Section 9):** Samoan hand-tapped tatau specifically is gendered and hereditary-practitioner-restricted (reserved for Samoan lineage and service, applied only by tufuga from specific family lines); Māori hand-tapped tā moko is sacred and actively protected, with kirituhi as the recognized non-restricted alternative for non-Māori interest. the catalogue should not generate designs claiming to be authentic Samoan or Māori hand-tapped work for non-community use.

**Skin_stitching carries a living-reclaimed-heritage consideration analogous to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s treatment of Marquesan patutiki:** the tradition is currently being actively reconstructed by Inuit and other Arctic Indigenous practitioners themselves after near-suppression, and external commercial or AI-generated use should avoid presenting skin-stitching-associated motifs (particularly tunniit facial-tattoo patterns) as a settled, freely available decorative vocabulary given that Indigenous practitioners are still in the process of recovering and re-establishing their own tradition's specific meanings and protocols.

## Critical Debates and Limits of Evidence

**Skin stitching's precise antiquity claim.** Sources reviewed for this paper variously date Inuit tattooing's practice to "at least 10,000 years" (Bering Straits Native Corporation source) without independent archaeological corroboration traced during this research pass; this figure is treated as community-attested/synthesized rather than independently verified direct archaeological evidence, distinguished from the more precisely dated and independently corroborated Ötzi evidence for Hand Poke (Stick & Poke) ([Functional & Material Exceptions](/blog/functional-and-material-exceptions)) or the documented Edo-period tebori lineage ([The Japanese Tattoo Continuum](/blog/the-japanese-tattoo-continuum)). Confidence: moderate on the general deep-antiquity claim; the specific "10,000 years" figure should be treated with appropriate caution pending dedicated archaeological sourcing.

**"Poke-and-tap" as a sub-variant within Skin Stitching's broader Arctic tradition.** At least one source documents a distinct "poke-and-tap" Inupiat technique (direct pigment tapping rather than threaded-needle stitching) as existing alongside skin stitching proper within the same broader Arctic tattooing tradition; this paper treats this as a related but mechanically distinct technique (functionally closer to Hand Poke (Stick & Poke)) rather than conflating it with Skin Stitching's specific threaded-needle mechanism, consistent with the corpus plan's instruction not to treat these manual technologies "as interchangeable."

**Source quality is notably strong for this paper's new Skin Stitching research.** This paper's skin-stitching material draws on a 2025 peer-reviewed-adjacent public health journal article (Canadian Institute of Public Health Inspectors), a 2016 CBC News profile, a Hakai Magazine feature citing a named Inuit needle-case expert, and a documented 1926 archaeological ethnographic account (Otto W. Geist) — a sourcing profile comparable in rigor to [Living Lineages: Indigenous & Heritage Systems](/blog/living-lineages-indigenous-and-heritage-systems)'s Samoan material (Mallon and Galliot) and stronger than the trade-blog-dependent sourcing typical of several other technique entries in this corpus.
